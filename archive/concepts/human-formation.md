## Introduction note — Refocus the university; do not dismantle it

**Captured from Jason's September 27 discussion.** Working argument for the introduction and student value proposition, not a new operating requirement or a completed research claim.

The falling cost of instruction does not make the institution of college irrelevant. It calls its organizing purpose into question.

Online learning already made it possible to assemble a substantial education outside a traditional university. In principle, learners could find a community and build their own school around available material. Jason's concern is that expecting young people to self-organize their own equivalent of a university overlooks one of the things they most need help developing: the social environment itself. Access to content does not automatically produce peers, sustained relationships, professional networks, mentorship, challenge, or the experience of becoming an adult among other people.

The argument is not that nobody has ever tried alternative or self-organized education. It is that cheap instruction alone is not a convincing replacement for the institution and its human functions.

Higher education has largely organized itself around transferring a defined knowledge corpus into the student's mind and proving that it is there. The scarcity value of access to that knowledge has been declining since the internet. AI goes further: it can index, retrieve, synthesize, and apply substantial portions of that knowledge when given direction. Building the whole institution around information transfer and recall therefore makes less sense as those capabilities become abundant.

This does not mean people no longer need knowledge or understanding. They need to be able to direct, question, apply, and take responsibility for it. The institution's purpose should follow that change.

College's enduring responsibility is to prepare young people for the world in which they will live and work. That world has changed. The institution should change how it fulfills its responsibility rather than defend its old instructional arrangement or abandon its social role.

The clearest explanation for a traditional educator or employer is: **take the first job and move it into college.**

The first job used to provide both useful output and professional formation. It taught people to work with others, build networks, test ideas, challenge assumptions, apply knowledge, exercise judgment, and carry responsibility. These capabilities are often dismissed as soft skills even though they become more important when knowledge production is inexpensive.

An employer may have little reason to pay a beginner to perform a research task that AI can complete for pennies. Jason's “$0.23” example expresses that economic logic; it is illustrative, not a measured price or universal cost claim. The employer's decision can be rational while still removing the environment in which beginners used to become experienced.

We already have an institution intended to prepare young people for their future. Refocus it on that missing developmental experience: real work, human relationships, judgment, application, challenge, and increasing responsibility, supported by AI and the university's people and facilities.

**We do not have to burn the university down. We have to change what it is organized to do.**

## AI should make the university more human, not less

Cheap personalized instruction does not make the physical institution irrelevant.

It changes what the institution should spend scarce human attention on.

The most valuable remaining university functions include:

- judgment;
- mentorship;
- relationships;
- peers;
- teams;
- social development;
- laboratories;
- equipment;
- professional networks;
- clubs and communities;
- conflict and collaboration;
- identity formation;
- active experts;
- consequential work;
- and the serendipity of living and learning around other ambitious people.

AI can absorb more of the repeatable instructional load so the institution can become more deliberately human.

The campus is not legacy overhead that survived the redesign.

It is part of the product.

## AI did not create the mismatch; it exposed a long-delayed redesign

Universities were originally organized around real scarcity:

- books were scarce;
- expert knowledge was scarce;
- explanation was scarce;
- laboratories were scarce;
- access to advanced information was scarce.

The internet made information abundant, but universities largely preserved the same instructional architecture.

AI now makes explanation, tutoring, synthesis, translation, practice, and routine intellectual production much more abundant as well.

The internet probably should have triggered a deeper redesign decades ago.

AI removes much of the remaining justification for treating information transfer as the university's organizing principle.

AI does not make the university unnecessary.

It makes information transfer increasingly unnecessary as the university's primary organizing function.

## Access-first education and institutional redesign solve different problems

A highly capable AI tutor can make world-class instruction available to a student who lacks access to a strong university.

That is important.

The historical lineage is roughly:

**One Laptop per Child → One AI per Child**

This can be transformative where the binding constraint is access, cost, geography, or institutional capacity.

University of Impact is solving a different problem:

> If students are still going to spend formative years together inside a physical institution with professors, peers, laboratories, equipment, clubs, networks, mentors, and industry access, what should that institution become in an AI economy?

An access-first alternative to college and an AI-native redesign of college can both be valuable.

University of Impact exists because the second problem remains unsolved.

## Networking is not an extracurricular benefit

The professional and social network is part of the education.

Students should not graduate and only then discover that relationships determine access to expertise, jobs, capital, customers, collaborators, and opportunities.

The university should make relationship-building a normal consequence of doing meaningful work with peers, mentors, faculty, visiting experts, alumni, and industry.

The goal is not to teach transactional networking tricks.

It is to create years of repeated, useful interaction in which students learn how to ask for help, offer value, maintain trust, and become credible to other people.

---

## The institution should preserve human formation

A strong university experience is not only an instructional delivery mechanism.

It is one of the last environments in which large numbers of young adults can spend several formative years surrounded by peers while experimenting with identity, relationships, leadership, independence, collaboration, and responsibility.

An AI-native redesign should not optimize those experiences away.

If human judgment, relationships, trust, collaboration, and social capability become more valuable as intelligence becomes cheaper, the university should deliberately produce more of them.

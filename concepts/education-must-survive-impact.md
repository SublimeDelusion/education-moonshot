# Education Must Survive Impact

**Students' ideas should encounter the world early enough for the world to change their education. Practicing people, unresolved problems, and external standards belong inside the learning process from the beginning.**

## Bring the outside world into education

A student can become very good at satisfying an assignment without encountering the constraints that make the same work difficult outside school. The model brings those constraints into view early.

An external practitioner can expose a missing assumption, a misunderstood user, a maintenance burden, or a professional standard the student did not know existed. The encounter gives the student a reason to revise both the work and their understanding.

This begins during exploration. It becomes more demanding as students develop expertise and reaches real commitments during the capstone years.

The student should learn to seek that contact. Work becomes stronger when someone with relevant experience can challenge it.

## Active experts can participate without becoming full-time professors

Many people with valuable current experience cannot teach a semester. The three-day Visiting Leaders Forum creates a bounded way to contribute.

The current design has three levels:

| Stage | Encounter |
| --- | --- |
| University-wide exchange | A leader brings frontier work, unresolved questions, and experience into the Forum. Students across years and disciplines participate. |
| Departmental engagement | Students developing the relevant profession examine methods, failures, tradeoffs, and decisions in greater depth. |
| Prepared working sessions | Students bring actual work, evidence of prior attempts, and a clear ask. Selected projects receive focused attention. |

The current operating model describes short morning appointments, shared lunch, and longer afternoon work with selected projects. Those details express the mechanism: preparation earns access to concentrated expertise.

The valuable result can be a correction, an introduction, an opened laboratory, a customer, a mentor, or a different problem to investigate.

## Prepared students make the encounter useful

Students should arrive able to show what they have attempted and identify where they need help. They rehearse with the Examiner, anticipate objections, and organize evidence.

The encounter is then followed by action. A useful suggestion changes the work. An introduction becomes a conversation. The student develops credibility by following through.

The expert gains a prepared counterpart rather than a vague request for inspiration. The student begins a relationship grounded in substance.

## The story shows the mechanism

During sophomore year, Anya asks a visiting engineer to identify the assumption most likely to make her system fail. He points out that maintenance has been treated as an external activity rather than part of the system.

That observation changes the architecture and directs further learning. An introduction to someone with Antarctic maintenance experience brings additional constraints into the work.

The scene illustrates how one concentrated encounter can alter a student's trajectory. It is narrative grounding for the mechanism, not evidence of a measured institutional outcome.

## Contact grows in consequence

In the early years, students encounter criticism, alternative explanations, and professional expectations. During founder preparation, they must defend customers, staffing, finance, and technical scope. Recruiting exposes whether colleagues trust the proposal enough to join it.

The Funding Forum adds external resource decisions. The Builder Year adds people who depend on delivery. By graduation, employers inspect the work and test the graduate on a new problem.

The institution deliberately increases consequence as capability develops. Its support makes ambitious attempts possible while keeping the student in contact with reality.

## Questions to develop

The model needs a sustainable supply of active practitioners, preparation standards, selection and scheduling, and follow-through. Access should reach across disciplines and students. The aim is repeated substantive engagement, not a calendar of prestigious names.

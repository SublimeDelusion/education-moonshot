When intelligence becomes abundant, education must help people decide what matters, develop the judgment to act, and learn to do consequential work with others.

Education Moonshot proposes a purpose-driven, AI-accelerated model for post-secondary education. Students explore before choosing a professional specialty, develop depth through application, and take responsibility for real work before graduation. Professors, peers, mentors, laboratories, and professional relationships are central to that experience.

The institution earns its value by bringing those people and opportunities together. AI expands what students can attempt and gives them more individual support. The student remains responsible for understanding, reasoning, and results.

University of Impact is the working name for this model. The institutional form and eventual name remain open. This is a proposed design under development.

Founding principles

1. Purpose determines what you learn

Students begin with something they want to accomplish. Their purpose drives the knowledge they pursue and the expertise they develop. A predetermined degree program no longer defines the boundaries of their education. Professional disciplines remain essential; students choose depth after exploring where their purpose leads.

2. Understanding determines how fast you learn

Students advance when they understand and receive focused support where they struggle. The pace of the class no longer determines the pace of their education. Shared instruction, laboratories, and collaboration remain valuable, while individualized tutoring allows each student to repair gaps or move ahead.

The student's purpose sets the direction. Their developing understanding sets the pace.

3. Replace the first rung of the career ladder

College provides the professional experience employers increasingly expect but no longer reliably offer beginners. Students exercise judgment, carry responsibility, and do consequential work before graduation. They leave ready to be evaluated as someone seeking their second professional role: through work performed, decisions owned, references, and demonstrated capability on a new problem.

4. Expand capability. Raise expectations.

Require AI and redesign the work around what students can now accomplish. Homework means applying a concept to a real problem. Testing means demonstrating that application, defending the reasoning, and adapting when conditions change. Recalling freely available information tells us little about the student or candidate; we need to see what they can do with it.

A research paper describing Japan's aging crisis becomes a proposed response: what would you do, why might it work, and how would you test it? Students need not solve a national crisis to demonstrate substantial understanding through a serious attempt.

Each student applies ideas to their own mission and brings the results back to the cohort. The same concept appears across different industries, constraints, and failures. Students teach, question, and learn from those applications. This is the living library of applications: AI expands what they can attempt, assessment demands meaningful application, and shared work deepens the cohort's understanding.

The student remains responsible for the reasoning and the result, including recognizing when an AI answer is wrong or beyond their understanding.

5. Your context library is your first professional asset

Students actively accumulate knowledge, sources, experiments, decisions, corrections, and the reasons their thinking changed. Their agent force learns the field, the work, and the person: how they frame problems, apply ideas, weigh tradeoffs, and need to be challenged.

This is an evolving professional relationship, not simply a collection of course materials. The accumulated context enables the agent force to function as an extension of the student's professional capabilities, even as models and tools change.

The performance record supplies evidence of capability; the accumulated working context is the asset. Graduates leave with a system that knows their work and knows how to work with them. Provenance, ownership, permissions, and portability remain explicit operating design questions.

6. Networking is not a soft skill

When access to knowledge becomes effectively free, access to trusted people becomes more consequential. Students deliberately develop the ability to find expertise, earn trust, contribute, assemble teams, and sustain relationships.

Cross-teaching, shared work, mentors, visiting experts, and alumni create repeated opportunities to practice. Graduates leave with people who know their work, whose capabilities they understand, and with whom they have earned the ability to work.

7. Education must survive early contact with reality

Bring practicing leaders, unresolved problems, competing perspectives, and external scrutiny into learning from day one. Students' ideas must encounter standards and constraints beyond the classroom.

Industry leaders who cannot teach a semester can invest three prepared, high-signal days in the next generation: a university-wide exchange, deeper departmental engagement, and working sessions with student teams. Students bring evidence, failed attempts, serious questions, and specific asks. Leaders challenge and guide the work, introduce professional standards, and open opportunities students could not otherwise access.

Salon culture sustains the conversation across disciplines. A formal talk can lead to direct critique, mentorship, laboratory access, a customer, a partnership, or funding. The outside world participates in creating the education. Contact begins early and grows in consequence through the four years.

8. Guarantee the opportunity. Students drive their own success.

The university provides access to instruction, mentors, tools, laboratories, networks, and meaningful work. Students are responsible for what they do with that access: their effort, judgment, initiative, and contribution.

A company's failure should not close off the opportunity to learn and perform. Institutional support keeps that opportunity open; it cannot guarantee learning, professional success, graduation, or a viable company. Capstone organizations are educational infrastructure, and their commercial outcomes are distinct from each student's development.

9. Success compounds across generations

Graduates return with expertise, customers, opportunities, relationships, and capital. Successful companies can strengthen the institution; unsuccessful ones can still leave useful research, equipment, methods, and hard-won lessons.

Each cohort begins with capabilities and relationships its predecessors had to build. Reuse follows the agreed ownership and access rules; institutional economics and exact equity terms remain open design work.
